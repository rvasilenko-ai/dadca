# Lecture 1 — Talk track

A spoken script for delivering *From Malware Files to Adversarial Capabilities*. It is written to be said aloud, one passage per slide, with the handover to the next slide as the last line of each. It is a guide, not a teleprompter: the slides carry the detail and the sources, this carries the through-line. Minute ranges match the deck; the last ten minutes are a question buffer.

The voice is first person to a room of undergraduate and Master's students. Square brackets are things to adapt on the day.

---

## 1 — Title · 0–1 min

Good morning. This is the first of three lectures on malware and AI. Today is about two things and how they fit together: how malware, and the malicious capabilities behind it, changed over twenty-five years, and how the systems we build to detect them had to change in step. The lecture has two halves — the attacker's side first, then the defender's — and one worked example runs through both, so you see the same attack from each direction. I won't assume you know Portable Executable internals, or MITRE ATT&CK, or machine learning; every term gets defined the first time it matters. First, who is talking.

## 2 — About me · 1–2 min

I have spent about twenty years on the detection side of this problem. I started on antivirus engines — unpacking, polymorphic viruses, reputation. Then Lastline, building a malware sandbox. Then VMware, on network detection. Then Mandiant, on breach simulation, which asks a sharp question: does the control you bought actually catch the attack? And now Talos. The point of this table is not the résumé. It is that my career happens to trace the same periods this lecture is about, and the sandbox years in the middle are the ones that bear most directly on today. Everything I say is my own reading; where I am wrong, the repository takes corrections. Now, before we agree on any terminology, I want you to classify five situations.

## 3 — Five suspicious situations · 2–4 min

Here are five situations, arranged as a ladder. At the top, a trojan written to disk — a file. At the bottom, an instruction in a repository that makes a coding agent leak secrets — no file at all, just data an agent reads. In between: PowerShell that downloads and runs code; a program that exists only in memory; and an attacker driving a legitimate remote-management tool. I am going to read them, and then I want a show of hands. Raise your hand at the last rung you would still call malware. [Read, count down from five.] Notice the room splits — a cluster near the top, a cluster near the bottom, and hesitation in the middle. I am not going to resolve it. [To one student near the top, one near the bottom:] what decided it for you? You will usually hear two different answers — "there is a file" versus "someone is doing harm." Hold on to that disagreement; it comes back at the end of Part 1. First, the route.

## 4 — Where we are going · 4–5 min

Two questions, one per column. Part 1 asks how malware and malicious capabilities evolved. Part 2 asks how detection pipelines are designed, and how they had to evolve. Part 1 is history — the attacker's side. Part 2 is the defender's side of the very same years, organised around a single pipeline. One thing I am deliberately keeping thin today: the detectors themselves — the machine learning, the LLM analyst. Those get their own lecture. Today is about what the detector is even allowed to see. So, why did malware become so strongly tied to files in the first place?

## 5 — Part 1: how the malicious capability outgrew the file · 5–6 min

One working definition, because the rest of Part 1 leans on it: malware is the malicious capability, whatever carries it. The file is one carrier. For about fifteen years it was effectively the only carrier, which is why "malware" and "file" fused in our heads. Part 1 is the attacker's side — what was delivered, why, and what changed around it. Keep in mind one claim I will keep returning to: the file stopped being the only unit, and it never actually left. Let us start with the model everything else grew from.

## 6 — The original mental model · 6–7 min

This is the original mental model, and I want to present it as a useful simplification, not as an error. A malicious executable arrives; a user or the system runs it; malicious behavior follows. When a stable file carries the capability, the defender has enormous leverage: you can intercept it before it runs, keep it, hash it, label it, share it with other vendors. The one assumption baked in is that the carrier is always a file. That was nearly true for fifteen years. The familiar malware taxonomy grew up around this object.

## 7 — The classical taxonomy · 7–9 min

Virus, worm, trojan, backdoor, spyware, rootkit. The thing to notice is that these words describe different dimensions of the same object. Trojan is about presentation. Worm is about propagation. Backdoor is about capability. Rootkit is about concealment. A single specimen can be all four at once — a worm that drops a trojan that installs a backdoor hidden by a rootkit. [Ask:] where does ransomware fit — is it an artifact type, a propagation method, a capability, or an objective? Hold that. These categories became operational because files were convenient security objects.

## 8 — Why files became the unit of detection · 9–11 min

Two reasons, and both matter. On the left, attackers used files because the victim's machine offered little else to abuse — email attachments, drive-by downloads, worms, bundled installers. The program was the capability, so the program was the right thing to catch. On the right is why the industry could build an entire discipline on that: files cross the boundary you control, they persist long enough to inspect, they hash identically everywhere, you can scan them before they run, and you can share samples between vendors and build labelled datasets. [Ask:] what did a 2005 attacker have to put on your machine to steal your documents — and what does a 2025 attacker have to put there? The gap between those two answers is the rest of this lecture. This model held for about fifteen years and then broke in stages. First, the whole span.

## 9 — Four periods, 2000–2026 · 11–13 min

Two minutes here, and I only want the shape. Five lanes running across the top: motive and money, attackers, the operating system, the internet, and the security products of each era. Four periods left to right. Start with money, because it explains the other four. [Name the lanes; read the four period names; one sentence per card.] A few of the dots are anchors you may recognise — Windows XP, Stuxnet, SolarWinds, Volt Typhoon — they are there so the zoomed slides have reference points. Two caveats. These are periods of the attacker's dominant tradecraft, not of famous incidents, and the boundaries are soft by a year or two. And the operating-system lane follows the Windows enterprise endpoint, because that is where the detection industry was built. The question for each period is simple: where did the capability go, and why did it move? Zoom into the first period.

## 10 — 2000–2009: the executable, from worms to botnets · 13–17 min

The first decade. Start with the machine: Windows XP, everyone an administrator, autorun on, firewall off until Service Pack 2. The attacker's unit is the executable, and it arrives by whatever works — a download, a worm hitting an unpatched service, a USB key, a browser exploit. The first half of the decade is the worms — Code Red, Slammer, Blaster, Sasser — spreading with no user action at all, until SP2 turns the firewall on. The second half is the botnets. The IRC bot families make the infected machine report to a channel and take orders, and that turns infections into inventory you can rent — for spam, for denial of service, for installs. Someone was actually prosecuted for exactly that trade. Motive explains the volume: the decade opens with worm authors chasing notoriety and closes with a criminal market, where the machine, its bandwidth, and what it types are the product. And notice the pattern that repeats for the rest of the lecture: every time a channel blocked the plain executable — mail in 2000, webmail in 2004 — the attackers answered within months, with archives, then links, then exploit documents. The next period starts with a different kind of adversary. The file stays, but it stops being only an executable.

## 11 — 2010–2016: APT, exploit kits, and files of every kind · 17–21 min

Three things happen here and they interact. First, the adversary changes: Aurora, Stuxnet, the APT1 report make the funded, persistent, mission-driven team the reference attacker. A file verdict tells you nothing about who is behind it or what they want — so the unit of analysis grows from the file to the campaign. Second, the money industrializes: exploit kits rented by the day, point-of-sale breaches, and then Bitcoin, which is the thing ransomware had been waiting for. CryptoLocker is the first campaign where the victim pays the attacker directly. Third, and I want you to be careful here, the file diversified but it did not disappear. Once mail blocked executable attachments, attackers changed the container — documents, scripts, exploit kits in the browser — not the fact of a file. A sandbox that detonates documents and scripts was exactly the right control for this period, which is why it became the premium purchase. The memory-only tradecraft is just appearing at the very end. One more thread: Snowden, in 2013, is why the whole internet started encrypting by default — remember that, it comes back in Part 2. The third period starts with a leak and ends with access itself as the commodity.

## 12 — 2017–2022: the access economy, identity, and the supply chain · 21–25 min

This period opens with the Shadow Brokers dumping state-grade exploits, and within weeks WannaCry and NotPetya show what happens when top-tier capability reaches everyone overnight. Then three shifts. Fileless becomes ordinary — not advanced tradecraft any more, but the default for criminal affiliates, and the share of intrusions that never drop a malware file climbs steadily right across this period. I will put a number on that two slides from now. The money moves from the consumer to the enterprise: the victim organization now pays the attacker directly, and the sum is whatever it costs to recover or to keep data private. Ryuk, then Maze with double extortion, then Colonial Pipeline, which made ransomware a national-security matter. And the traded commodity becomes access itself — stolen through identities, injected through trusted vendors like SolarWinds. EDR became the control that mattered in this period, and you can tell, because attackers started bringing vulnerable signed drivers specifically to switch it off. The current period keeps the same capabilities and can run with no file at all. The file does not disappear; it stops being required.

## 13 — 2023–2026: hands-on hacking, edge devices, and agents · 25–29 min

The newest period. Volt Typhoon is a whole campaign run on the organization's own built-in tools and valid accounts — nothing to scan on the host. Scattered Spider reaches large enterprises with a phone call to the help desk, not an exploit. And the agent incidents, where an instruction planted in data becomes the thing that executes. Say this precisely: "no malware file," never "no malware." The hands-on intrusion, the built-in tools, the stolen session — those are the malicious capability; what is gone is the file that used to carry it. But I want to be just as careful the other way. Most campaigns still start with a file. The implants and infostealers sold on criminal markets are files — now written in Go, Rust and .NET, some with an LLM call inside. And the oldest business model came back: XCPE, disclosed just [last week], is the botnet-for-hire of 2005, except the backdoor is in the firmware of more than a million set-top boxes nobody will ever patch — found from the network side, and answered by the platform owner, not by any endpoint product. Now put the four periods back on one list: what broke the file-centric model, and when.

## 14 — What broke the file-centric model, and when · 29–31 min

Everything you have just seen, as one table of breaks, ordered by when each became dominant. Read the middle callout and the number beside it. Here is the number I promised: the malware-free share of detections went from 40 percent in 2018 to nearly 80 percent in 2024. That is the thesis of Part 1 as a single figure. One vendor's telemetry, so trust the direction, not the decimal. Now the causal point, because the evidence is finally on the table: file defenses worked. Signatures and reputation made an unknown executable expensive, so attackers diversified the file. Sandboxes made the unknown document expensive, so they went to memory and built-in tools. EDR made implants visible, so they went to identity and vendors. Each row is partly a consequence of winning the row above it. Two things that did not change: the person is still the way in, and the file never actually left — it stopped being required, not rare. [Ask:] which row is the first where a perfect file classifier, with a perfect signature database, would have had nothing useful to classify? [Then, in one breath, map the five opening situations onto the rows.] When the file is no longer the unit, we need a vocabulary for behavior instead.

## 15 — MITRE ATT&CK: what it is · 31–33 min

ATT&CK is a public, curated knowledge base of adversary behavior, built from real intrusions, maintained by MITRE, and versioned every April and October. Three columns. What is in it: tactics, techniques, and for each technique the real campaigns that used it, the groups, the mitigations, the data you would need to detect it. What it is for: a vocabulary that survives attackers swapping one tool for another that does the same thing — plus coverage mapping, intelligence, red and purple teaming. And what it is not, because students over-read it: it is not a complete list of everything an attacker could do, the tactics are goals rather than a required order, and — this is the bridge to Part 2 — a technique ID names a behavior; it is not evidence that the behavior happened. Here is what the matrix looks like, and how to read it.

## 16 — From names to behaviors: ATT&CK's levels · 33–37 min

Columns are tactics — the adversary's goals, in the order a campaign tends to need them. Cells are techniques — the behaviors that achieve a goal. The expanded column shows the third level, sub-techniques. The count under each header is the real number of techniques in that tactic; the matrix moves, and it has fifteen tactics since Defense Evasion split in April 2026. Now watch — [Next] — a classic trojan lights up as a path: a phished attachment, a packed binary that injects into another process, a Run key for persistence, web command-and-control, and encryption for impact. Seven techniques across seven tactics. Process Injection lights in two columns because the same behavior serves two goals — that is the matrix's whole point. The word "trojan" said less than this path. And every lit cell is a place a sensor could look, which is the question Part 2 is about. A tool is an implementation vehicle, not the behavior itself. Let me show you two cases where the software is not just legitimate but byte-for-byte unchanged.

## 17 — Capabilities delivered through benign files · 37–40 min

Same tool, different procedures; same technique, different tools. So where does "malicious" reside when the artifact itself is legitimate and unchanged? [Next — card one.] A remote-management tool, operated by the wrong person. It works exactly as designed; what differs is who controls it and whose objective it serves. [Next — card two.] A signed but vulnerable driver. Here there is a second difference — not just who loads it, but what its flaw lets them do. [Next — the answer.] As files, both are identical to their legitimate copies. A file classifier sees two clean, signed programs. What makes them malicious lives outside the file: who is in control and to what end, and, for the driver, what its flaw allows. Now apply this vocabulary to an attack that never produces a malware file.

## 18 — Map an attack with no malware file · 40–45 min

Here is the scenario that will follow us through Part 2. A stolen administrator account runs PowerShell. A script downloads a legitimate, signed but vulnerable driver, loads it, and uses it to switch off the endpoint protection from the kernel. It then downloads a memory-only implant, injects it into explorer.exe, and uses it to find documents and upload them. Take two minutes in pairs and map it on the empty matrix — artifacts, tools, techniques, objective, and which sensor would see each step. [Reveal the eight steps.] Notice two things. The only file written to disk is a legitimate signed driver — the malicious capability never appears as a malware file, which is exactly the benign-driver case from the last slide, now inside a full chain. And "no malware file" does not mean no evidence: there are three network crossings and a dozen host events. [Close the vote, from memory.] All five situations from the start were malware — malicious capability in use. What the room disagreed about were four different questions asked with one word: what it is, what it can do, who controls it, and whose objective it serves. That is Part 1. Part 2 asks the fifth question — what a sensor can see of any of it — and how the pipelines that answer it were built.

## 19 — Part 2: how detection pipelines are built, and how they had to change · 45–46 min

One sentence of signposting. Part 1 described the attack. Part 2 is the design and evolution of the detection pipeline, and I am going to do it history first: the stages, then the two pipelines rebuilt generation by generation, then the stage each attacker change broke, then the menu as it stands today, then six misses to diagnose. The PowerShell attack you just mapped is the test case; each pipeline ends with what its generations would have caught of it. Part 1 closed with four questions about the thing. Part 2 asks the fifth: what can a sensor see of any of it, and what has to happen before a detector sees anything?

## 20 — The complete detection pipeline · 46–49 min

This is the whole pipeline, and I will step through it, about twenty seconds a stage. Sensor: can the system observe the event at all? Acquisition: is it collected and kept, and for how long? Extraction: can you recover something analyzable from it? Representation: what does the model actually receive — and what got thrown away before it arrived? Detection: the one stage most people mean by the word. Investigation: can you connect and explain it. Decision: can you choose an action. Response: can you carry it out in time. [Step through; the panel shows an example or two per stage.] The whole of Part 2 is this legend, so do not over-study it now. The one line to keep: a detector cannot reason about evidence it never receives. Every stage here is a choice, and the choices were made in history. The host first — three generations of the same pipeline.

## 21 — Host and file lineage: antivirus, sandbox, EDR · 49–55 min

Three generations, and nothing was retired — today's endpoint agent is EDR with the antivirus engine inside it, and a sandbox both of them can call. Walk two transitions. Antivirus to sandbox: antivirus already reached for unknown files with heuristics and emulation, but as a blocking product it has a false-positive budget, and extraction kept failing on packed and novel files — so the industry added a generation that runs the file instead of parsing it. The sandbox has no sensor of its own; it borrows the gateway's and asks for the file. Sandbox to EDR: once the capability is an inline command or a memory-only implant, nothing arrives to detonate, and you cannot fix an empty first stage further down. The answer was the host, and a different premise — assume breach. EDR takes the same sensor as antivirus but forwards, retains and correlates the data instead of discarding it, so you can reconstruct and hunt after the fact. That is why it survived the attacker's move off files: it never depended on a file. It is a different control, not a next-generation antivirus. [Last step — the scenario check.] Against our attack, antivirus and the sandbox see only a legitimate signed driver; EDR would record the whole chain and fire on the sequence — which is precisely why the attacker's first move is to disable it from the kernel. The kernel tampering becomes the thing to detect. The network sensor went through the same decade — except there the evidence was taken away not by the attacker, but by encryption.

## 22 — The network changed under the sensor · 55–57 min

Network intrusion detection was built when the web was plaintext: you matched exploit strings and downloaded executables in the payload. Then, in about five years, encrypted page loads went from roughly 40 percent to roughly 90 percent — and the reason, remember, was Snowden and then Let's Encrypt making certificates free. Watch the sequence, because it matters as much as the first step. HTTPS took the payload. TLS 1.3 took the certificate. DNS over HTTPS took the lookup. Encrypted Client Hello takes the last field, the server name. A payload signature cannot match content it never sees, and the window has been closing one field at a time. That is the change. Now what it did to each stage of the network pipeline.

## 23 — Network lineage: what each stage lost · 57–60 min

This is the twin of the host table, with one crucial difference: the sensor stayed exactly where it was. The appliance still sees every packet; it simply cannot open them any more. So what failed was extraction, and everything below it had to be rebuilt on metadata or moved to the endpoint. Defenders had three answers: decrypt in the middle with a proxy — which breaks pinning and becomes a high-value target; move to metadata — endpoints, timing, TLS fingerprints — which answers different questions with less precision; or move the sensor to the endpoint, where the plaintext still exists before it is encrypted. And be precise about what survived: internal east-west traffic is often still inspectable, scanning and beacon timing are still visible. [Scenario check.] In our attack, three moments cross the network — the driver download, the implant download, the upload — all inside TLS, so a content signature matches nothing, but the connections, their timing and their reputation remain, and the endpoint sees the plaintext. Two pipelines, one pattern: the evidence moved and the sensor followed. Now every change from Part 1 on one list, with the stage it broke and the product that answered.

## 24 — Where each change broke the pipeline · 60–64 min

This is slide 14's list read from the defender's side, now that you have seen both lineages — with two rows added, packing from the file era and encryption from the network side. Each row names the stage that broke, the evidence that left its view, and the product generation that answered. Two points. Four of the seven rows broke at the sensor, and in every case the fix landed at whatever stage broke — a new sensor, decryption at extraction, a better feature at representation. And say this plainly: the products did fail. Antivirus missed packed files, the sandbox missed memory-only payloads, the IDS missed everything inside TLS, EDR misses the stolen token. In each case the product did exactly what it was built to do, and the evidence was no longer where it looked. Nothing was retired, but the premium control of one decade became the commodity of the next — and this is the normal condition of the field, not a story with an ending. There is no final round. Every row ended with a product. Here is the whole menu as it stands today — every line a product of that history.

## 25 — Options at each stage · 64–68 min

I will not read fifty cards. Step to two stages and name one trade-off for the rest. The sensor row is the one the lineages were about — each product class you just saw is one or two lines here, and the history is the industry adding lines as the capability moved. The response row is the one students forget: isolating a host does nothing against a stolen token, and revoking a token does nothing against a hijacked agent unless the harness lets you take its tools away. One thing to settle, because it comes up every time: signatures and machine learning are not file-only methods. The same engine that matches bytes in a file matches rules over memory in an EDR and over packets in an IDS. What is file-centric is the public evidence base — the datasets — not the methods. Use this slide as the answer to "why don't you just do X": almost every X is a row here, with its blind spot in the last column. Six misses to diagnose next. Every answer is a row on this slide — and "we need better AI" is not one of them.

## 26 — Diagnostic exercise · 68–75 min

Six cases. For each, tell me which stage of the pipeline failed, in the pipeline's own words, and whether retraining the classifier would help. [Hands per case, then reveal.] A and B are sensor failures — a memory payload the file scanner never sees, an HTTPS transfer the plaintext signature never reads. C is a representation or classifier failure — the file was parsed fine and scored wrong; that one retraining might touch. D is investigation — every sensor worked, every alert was right, and nobody connected them. E is decision — the right action existed and was not authorized in time. F is response — the decision was correct and could not be carried out. Two things to take away. Only some of these are repaired by a bigger model, so name the failed stage before you reach for one. And a detection is not an outcome: half of these misses happened after a correct detection. Every answer was a row on the options slide. Five conclusions.

## 27 — Five conclusions · 75–80 min

[Recall the close of the vote in one sentence: all five were malicious capability in use; they differed in the carrier and in which sensor could see them.] Five conclusions. One: the capability outgrew the file because file defenses worked and the money moved — and the file stayed, as one carrier among several. Two: software identity is not the same as adversary behavior. Three: detection begins with visibility and extraction, not with the classifier. Four: every product observes one unit of evidence, and when the evidence moves it keeps catching what stayed and misses what left, so a new layer is added. Five: a detection is not an outcome — investigation, decision and response are stages too, and half of tonight's misses were there. The through-line: better reasoning cannot recover evidence that was never observed, or that was destroyed on the way to the model. That is where Lecture 3 begins — with the prioritization that expensive reasoning forces. I would rather not end on "any questions." I would rather you answer one: what will the next sensor have to see?
