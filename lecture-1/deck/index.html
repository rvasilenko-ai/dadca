From malicious programs to adversarial capabilitiesmin 10–12

Planned 10–12 minutes · From malicious programs to adversarial capabilities

Start with the left column. It is the first two periods on slides 10 and 11: the file era proper, and then the period in which the file diversified into documents, scripts and exploit objects but remained the deliverable. For roughly fifteen years the attacker's problem was to get their own code onto the victim's machine and run it, because the victim's machine offered little else to work with. PowerShell did not ship by default until Windows 7 in 2009, remote-management tooling was not ubiquitous, and business data lived on the host rather than behind cloud APIs. Email attachments, drive-by downloads, removable media, worms, and bundled installers all delivered a program. The program was the capability, so the program was the right thing to catch.

The right column is why the industry could build on that. Files cross the boundary the defender controls, persist long enough to inspect, hash identically everywhere, and can be scanned before they run. Samples flow between vendors, signature databases grow, sandboxes detonate them, and supervised datasets are built from them. Connect this to antivirus hooks, sample exchanges, and later the public PE datasets covered in Lecture 3.

Stop here. Do not preview how the model breaks; the next four slides show it period by period, and slide 14 draws the list. The only forward-looking sentence this slide needs is that the model held for about fifteen years.

Read the five tracks as one system, starting with the money. The attacker's unit was the executable because the victim ran as administrator on a machine with no built-in tooling worth abusing, received email and browsed with plug-in-heavy browsers, and the network carried everything in the clear. The defenses that mattered were exactly those built on that unit: file scanners, payload signatures, spam filters.

Motive explains the volume. The decade opens with worm authors seeking notoriety and closes with a criminal market. Adware and pay-per-install paid per infected machine; spam botnets rented out capacity; the same botnets sold DDoS, first as extortion of online businesses and then as a service for hire (Ancheta, indicted in November 2005 for selling botnet access for DDoS and spam, was the first US prosecution of its kind); click fraud monetized browsers; banking trojans and carding monetized credentials. Once an infection was worth a few cents to a few dollars, the attacker's problem became scale, and scale is what produced botnets and server-side polymorphism. The product was the victim's machine, its bandwidth and what it typed.

Within the decade the attacker moved three times inside the file:

  • Packers and protectors made the bytes on disk stop matching the code that ran. Antivirus added emulation and generic unpacking: the scanner ran the file a little to see it.
  • Rootkits and bootkits made the live system lie about which files and processes existed. The Sony BMG case in 2005 made the technique public. Defenders inspected raw disk and memory from outside the lying layer, and Microsoft's 64-bit kernel-patch protection (2005) and Vista's mandatory driver signing (2007) were in part a response.
  • Botnets and server-side polymorphism turned the host into a rented commodity and gave every victim a unique binary. The asset was the command-and-control infrastructure; the binary was disposable. This broke hash and byte signatures operationally and produced reputation and prevalence systems (McAfee Artemis in 2008, Symantec Quorum and Insight in 2009–2010), cloud lookups, generic detections, and pushed machine learning on file features from research (first papers in 2001) toward products (from about 2012). Lecture 3 picks that up.

The operating-system row explains why so much was possible: administrator by default, autorun, and no exploit mitigations until SP2 and Vista. Vista's UAC and driver signing were unpopular precisely because they took privileges away from the model attackers had been enjoying.

Crime industrializes into a service economy: exploit kits are licensed by the year or rented by the day, installs are bought and sold, and point-of-sale malware turns retail breaches into card data. Bitcoin, usable at scale from about 2013, is the enabler that ransomware had been waiting for: CryptoLocker is the first campaign in which the victim pays the attacker directly rather than the attacker monetizing the victim's machine through a third party. At the same time the APT era brings an adversary whose payoff is strategic, not financial: espionage, intellectual property, sabotage. The same tradecraft serves both, which is one reason a file verdict cannot tell you what you are dealing with. North Korea's Bangladesh Bank heist in 2016 shows a state using the APT toolkit for money.

Three more shifts happen in this period and they interact.

The adversary changed. Operation Aurora in January 2010, Stuxnet that June and the APT1 report in 2013 made the funded, persistent, mission-driven team the reference adversary. Multi-stage tooling, zero-days, stolen signing certificates, months of dwell. A file verdict says nothing about who is behind it or what they want. The kill chain paper in 2011, threat intelligence as a discipline, and ATT&CK in 2015 are the defensive response: the campaign, not the file, becomes the unit of analysis. That is the next module.

The file diversified; it did not disappear. Be careful not to overstate this period. The main deliverable was still a file. What changed was the kind of file and the channel, and the cause was defensive. Outlook had blocked dangerous attachment types since 2000 and mail gateways followed, so by the late 2000s an executable attachment rarely reached a user. Attackers diversified the container instead: archives wrapping executables, PDF and Office exploit documents, macro documents again from 2014, JavaScript, VBScript and HTA downloaders, shortcut files, and the browser through exploit kits. This was itself a second wave; macro viruses from 1995 and the VBScript worms of 2000–2001 had shown that a document or script could carry the capability before Office macro security and attachment blocking pushed attackers back to executables for a decade. Exploit kits delivered through Flash, Java and Internet Explorer and then dropped an executable, so the first artifact was an exploit object rather than an attachment. Malicious PDFs and Office exploits, then macro documents from 2014 when plug-ins declined, carried the capability in a document. Scripts, archives and JARs replaced the bare executable in email. Ransomware arrived as a file. A sandbox that detonated documents, archives, scripts and the executables they dropped was therefore the right control for this period, which is why it became the premium control around 2013 to 2016.

The exit from the file begins at the end. Windows 7 had put PowerShell on every machine. PowerSploit in 2012, Cobalt Strike the same year and Empire in 2015 made reflective loading and memory-only payloads a framework feature, Poweliks and Kovter persisted in the registry with no executable on disk, and Mimikatz made stolen credentials cheaper than exploits. The term "living off the land" was coined in 2013. In this period these were the tradecraft of advanced operators and red teams. They become standard in the next period, and that is when the sandbox is starved. Slide 25 covers the mechanism.

Microsoft's AMSI and script block logging in 2015 are the operating system moving the sensor to where the capability went. EDR and Sysmon are the product market doing the same, but note the premise: EDR was named in 2013 because defenders had accepted that antivirus would not stop a determined adversary and that breaches had to be detected and investigated after the fact. It is a different control, not a next-gen antivirus. Slide 25 develops this.

Antivirus still matters: it is the prevention layer of that agent, and it still stops most commodity malware before anything else sees it. Each later generation was added because a kind of evidence had left the previous one's view, not because the previous one stopped working.

Use two stages to explain why each next generation exists. Antivirus to sandbox: antivirus already reached for unknown files and unknown families with heuristics, generic detections and emulation, but as a preventive control it had to do so at a false-positive rate a blocking product can afford, and the extraction and detection rows still failed on packed, polymorphic and novel files, so the industry added a generation that runs the file instead of parsing it. The sandbox added no sensor of its own; it borrowed the gateway's and the endpoint's and asked them for the file. Sandbox to EDR: because the sandbox depends on another product handing it a self-contained object, once the capability was an inline command, a staged payload or a memory-only implant nothing arrived to detonate, and nothing further down a pipeline can repair an empty first row. The answer was the host, where antivirus's hooks already were: EDR took the same sensor and the same collection and changed what happens to the data. Instead of being judged on the host and discarded, it is forwarded, retained and correlated centrally, so detection, investigation, decision and response were rebuilt around a record of activity rather than a verdict on an object. The difference between the two columns starts below the acquisition row, and the decision row carries the preventive-versus-detective difference: antivirus and the sandbox render a verdict on an object before it runs, EDR records activity and alerts after the fact.

Around 2016, the network and mail sandbox was the premium control. It solved the real problem of its time: judging an object by what it does rather than by what it looks like, so that exploit documents, zero-days and novel implants arriving by mail and web were caught regardless of packing, polymorphism or signature coverage. Detonate it, record the behavior, and decide.

The sandbox needs a self-contained, detonatable artifact. Walk through what starves it, using the recurring scenario where possible:

  • Inline commands. The PowerShell one-liner is text in a process command line. There is no file to submit.
  • Staged payloads. A stager fetches its second stage only from the real victim. The command-and-control server is geo-fenced, rate-limited, or offline when the sandbox asks. The sandbox records a harmless download attempt.
  • DLL side-loading. The malicious DLL only runs when the legitimate signed host executable loads it from a specific directory layout, sometimes with a specific argument or environment. Submit the DLL alone and nothing happens.
  • Memory-only implants. The implant never exists as a file on any system. The only artifact the sandbox could receive is the loader, and the loader is often legitimate.
  • Evasion. Sleeps, environment checks, user-interaction gating, and hardware fingerprinting detect the analysis VM itself. The VM was a generic image, rarely anything like the victim's machine, which is also why environment-keyed payloads did nothing in it.

EDR came from a different premise. By the early 2010s, after Aurora, years of antivirus misses and breach reports measuring dwell time in months (Mandiant's median was 416 days for 2011 and 243 for 2012), defenders accepted that a determined adversary would get past prevention. The response was "assume breach": if prevention will fail, the control that matters is the one that lets you detect and investigate the compromise afterward. The term endpoint detection and response was coined in 2013 for tools built on that premise. Antivirus and the sandbox are preventive controls that render a verdict on an object before it runs. EDR is a detective and investigative control over activity: it records process ancestry, command lines, script content, memory regions, module loads, network connections and the identity behind them, whether or not anything was blocked, so that an analyst can reconstruct what happened, hunt for what was missed, and contain it remotely. Dwell time, which antivirus never measured, is the metric EDR was built against.

This is also why EDR survived the attacker's departure from files: it never depended on a file. The production host became the place where real execution is observed, and containment and rollback were added behind pre-execution judgment. Students who think of EDR as "next-generation antivirus" miss that the two answer different questions: "should this object run?" versus "what has already happened on this host?"

Be precise on the slide. The sandbox did not become useless. It became a commodity capability with a narrower role: commodity droppers and malicious documents at machine scale, and detonation as one tool among several at investigation scale. Lecture 3 returns to it as a tool an analyst agent calls.

EDR has its own starvation column. It sees the host. It does not see the attacker's session in a SaaS console, a cloud API call made with a stolen token, or a legitimate remote-management tool doing exactly what it was designed to do. That is where identity telemetry, and the control and objective questions from slide 21, take over.

On the detection stage, say what the EDR's memory telemetry actually gives, using the recurring scenario. PowerShell runs on .NET, and the CLR legitimately JIT-compiles intermediate language into native code in process memory; browsers, JVMs, emulators and instrumentation do the same. So executable private memory, or a protection change, is observable and is not a verdict: visibility is not distinguishability, and distinguishability is not a verdict. The defender needs context the representation has to keep, identity and process ancestry, command and script content, network provenance, the memory region's provenance and backing, control transfers and thread context, follow-on behavior. Once the sensor sat on the host, the problem did not disappear; it moved from sensor and acquisition to representation and detection, which is why Case A in the exercise is a sensor failure and Case C a classifier one.

As a rhetorical question, not an exercise: which column of the recurring scenario would each product have captured, the command line, the shellcode, the document access, or the upload? The EDR captures the most and still cannot say "malicious" from any single event.

Read the table as the twin of the host and file table, with one difference: the sensor row stayed where it was. What failed was extraction. The appliance still sees every packet and can no longer open them, so everything below extraction had to be rebuilt on metadata, and the content-level questions moved to the endpoint and the identity provider. What encryption hides from payload inspection (strings, transferred code and documents, application commands, content signatures, archive contents) remains observable somewhere else: endpoints and DNS activity, timing and volume, TLS properties, the process-to-connection relationship, and the plaintext on the endpoint itself. Next and Previous step through the stages one at a time, with the two eras side by side; clicking a stage on the strip jumps to it, and the table button shows the whole matrix.

Defenders had three responses:

  1. Decrypt in the middle. A TLS inspection proxy restores payload visibility for north-south web traffic, but it breaks certificate pinning, raises privacy and legal questions, costs performance, and becomes a high-value target holding every session in plaintext.
  2. Move to metadata. Network detection and response products use endpoints, timing, volume, and TLS fingerprints such as JA3 and JA4. These answer different questions with lower specificity.
  3. Move the sensor to the endpoint. The plaintext still exists where the process encrypts and decrypts it. EDR observes the process, the connection, and the content before encryption.

Be precise about what survived. East-west internal traffic is often still plaintext or inspectable: SMB, Kerberos, LDAP, and remote-management protocols, which is exactly where lateral movement and credential abuse happen. Scanning, DNS without encryption, and beacon timing remain visible. North-south payload signatures are what lost most of their value without decryption.

In the recurring scenario, TLS may conceal the downloaded payload and the uploaded content from an intermediate network sensor. The endpoint may still observe PowerShell, the network retrieval, the document access and the memory activity; metadata and endpoint context answer different questions and often with lower specificity.

Diagnostic exercise

Case A

A memory-resident payload is missed by an on-write file scanner.

Sensor and acquisition mismatch. Memory or behavioral evidence is needed; this is the antivirus and sandbox column of the host and file lineage. Retraining does not help.

Case B

An HTTPS transfer is missed by a plaintext content signature.

Payload visibility mismatch at the sensor. Metadata or endpoint evidence may help; this is the network lineage. Retraining does not help.

Case C

A retained PE file is parsed successfully but receives a benign score.

Representation or classifier failure. Retraining may be relevant, after checking labels and distribution shift.

Case D

Three low-severity alerts on three systems over two days: an unusual sign-in, a PowerShell process, a proxy connection. Each is closed as noise.

Investigation failure. Every alert was right; the evidence was never connected. Correlation, not a better classifier.

Case E

The alert is correct and investigated, but isolating the server needs an approval that is unreachable overnight. The upload completes.

Decision failure. An appropriate action existed and was not authorized in time. Fix the authorization design, not the evidence.

Case F

Isolation is ordered, but the laptop is off the VPN and the EDR cannot reach it for six hours.

Response failure. The decision was correct and could not be executed. Containment has to work where the host actually is.

For each: which pipeline stage failed? What additional evidence is needed? Would retraining help?

Five conclusions

  1. Attackers left the file because file defenses worked and because the money moved.
